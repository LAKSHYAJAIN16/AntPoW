AntChain is a Bitcoin-style consensus mechanism where every block requires miners to find a good route through a small delivery-style routing problem — using real ant colony optimization — and a real SHA-256 hash below a target that gets easier the better that route is. This page walks through why that's harder to get right than it sounds, and what actually happened when we built and ran it three separate ways.

01 · The tension

Bitcoin's uselessness is not an accident

Every SHA-256 hash attempt in Bitcoin mining is an independent coin flip. That sounds wasteful — and it is — but it's also precisely what makes the system secure: whoever controls twice the hashrate wins blocks roughly twice as often, with no exceptions for cleverness, luck, or prior knowledge. Replace the coin flip with something that can be gamed — a computation with memory, or one where being early or clever pays off — and that clean guarantee is the first thing to go.

Ant Colony Optimization looks like an appealing computation to fold in: it's a population of simulated ants probabilistically building candidate routes, reinforcing the good ones with a shared "pheromone" trail — genuinely useful work, and naturally distributed, almost like a mining swarm already. The catch is in that word reinforcing: a pheromone trail is memory. A miner who has searched longer, or who reuses a trail from a similar problem, carries a real advantage into the next attempt. Memory is exactly what a hash coin-flip is designed not to have.

02 · The naive version

Just let the best route win? It breaks three ways.

The obvious design — first miner to submit a good-enough route wins the block, no hashing at all — fails for three separate reasons.

FAILURE 01

Private knowledge

A miner who solved a similar instance before, or hears about a new block a few milliseconds early, starts the next round already ahead — with no way for anyone else to tell.

FAILURE 02

Pheromone memory

Within a single round, early progress compounds: a colony that stumbles onto a good partial route reinforces it, making further improvement cheaper for whoever's already ahead. Hash search has no such rich-get-richer effect.

FAILURE 03

Strategic withholding

Find a great route early? Sit on it until the last possible moment to deny everyone else time to react. There's no proof of elapsed effort attached — only proof of quality — so waiting is nearly free.

!

We didn't just argue this — we measured it. Across every population size and setting we tested, letting solution quality decide the winner outright produced a fork on essentially every single block. See the data in section 04.

03 · The mechanism

Let quality bias the lottery. Don't let it replace the lottery.

The fix keeps the hash coin-flip as the thing that actually grants a block — preserving Bitcoin's security argument — while making the difficulty of that coin-flip a continuous function of how good a miner's route is. A better route buys more lottery tickets. It never buys the block outright.

The honest part. We expected hybrid's fairness gap to sit near SHA-PoW's (0.10) and land somewhere between it and pure PoAO's (0.39) depending on λ. Instead, at 200 miners, it sits right next to pure PoAO's — regardless of λ. The reason: a miner's search budget is proportional to its hashrate share, so under a realistic (heavy-tailed) hashrate distribution, most low-share miners never accumulate enough search iterations to even qualify a route — they're excluded before the hash lottery ever runs, and no amount of λ-tuning fixes an exclusion that happens upstream of it. This only showed up once the C++ implementation made a 200-miner sweep fast enough to run; it's invisible at 20–30 miners. It's now the paper's main open problem.
